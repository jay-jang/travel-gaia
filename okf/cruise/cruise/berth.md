> In cruise and maritime industry usage, "berth" has two distinct meanings: (1) a bed or sleeping place within a stateroom (a cabin described as "two-berth" accommodates two passengers in two beds; upper and lower berths describe bunk arrangements); and (2) a dock or mooring position at a port where the cruise ship is secured. Cruise lines, port authorities, and regulators use the term in both senses, making context essential.

As sleeping accommodation, berths are the fundamental passenger capacity unit for cruise ships: a vessel is described by its number of lower berths rather than staterooms, because cabins with upper/lower bunk arrangements or a pull-out sofa berth can accommodate more passengers than a double stateroom count would suggest. The metric Passenger Space Ratio is calculated on lower berths. Berth occupancy — the proportion of a ship's berths occupied on a voyage — is the cruise analogue of hotel occupancy. As a dock location, a berth at a homeport includes dedicated terminal facilities; at a port of call it may be a quay berth or a tender berth requiring tender transfer.

# Distinctions
- **Berth** vs Stateroom — A stateroom is the complete cabin room on the ship (walls, door, amenities, bathroom); a berth is the individual sleeping place within that stateroom — a single stateroom can contain multiple berths.
- **Berth** vs Tender Port — A tender port is a port of call where no quay berth is available, requiring tender boats for shore access; the "berth" in its dock sense distinguishes quay-berth stops from tender-only stops.
