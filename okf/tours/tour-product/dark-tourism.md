> Dark tourism is travel to sites associated with death, tragedy, suffering, or historical atrocity — such as battlefields, Holocaust memorials, disaster zones, former prisons, and sites of genocide or mass-casualty events. First defined academically by J. John Lennon and Malcolm Foley in 1996 (and elaborated in their 2000 book), it is now a widely recognised and growing niche within heritage and cultural tourism, studied by the Institute for Dark Tourism Research (IDTR) at the University of Central Lancashire.

Lennon and Foley (2000) situated dark tourism within the condition of postmodernity, arguing that media-saturated societies have developed a commodified fascination with death and disaster. Well-known dark tourism destinations include Auschwitz-Birkenau (Poland), the Killing Fields and Tuol Sleng Museum (Cambodia), the 9/11 Memorial (USA), the Chernobyl Exclusion Zone (Ukraine), Alcatraz (USA), and war cemeteries across Europe and the Asia-Pacific. Philip Stone, director of the IDTR, developed a spectrum typology — from "darkest" (genocide camps, atrocity sites) to "lightest" (battlefields and heritage exhibitions with nostalgic framing) — recognising that sites vary in their intent, management, and the darkness of experience supplied. Visitor motivations include education and history-seeking, commemorative remembrance, empathy, existential reflection, and — more controversially — morbid curiosity. For destination managers and tour operators, dark tourism raises important ethical questions: how to balance reverence, educational interpretation, and commercial viability at sites of suffering; how to involve and compensate local communities; and how to avoid the commodification of trauma.

# Distinctions
- **Dark Tourism** vs [Ecotourism](/tours/tour-product/ecotourism.md) — Ecotourism centres on experiencing and conserving natural environments and biodiversity, typically with a positive and restorative framing; Dark Tourism centres on sites of human tragedy, death, or suffering, with an educational, commemorative, or reflective framing. Both are cultural tourism niches but serve entirely different motivational and experiential purposes.
- **Dark Tourism** vs [Culinary Tourism](/tours/tour-product/culinary-tourism.md) — Culinary Tourism is driven by positive sensory and cultural engagement with food and beverage heritage; Dark Tourism is driven by education, remembrance, empathy, or existential contemplation at sites of tragedy. Both are special-interest subsets of cultural tourism but with entirely different experiential registers.
- **Dark Tourism** vs [Adventure Tourism](/tours/tour-product/adventure-tourism.md) — Adventure Tourism seeks physical challenge and excitement in natural or remote settings; Dark Tourism seeks historical understanding, empathy, and contemplation at sites of human tragedy. Physical challenge plays no role in most dark tourism experiences.
